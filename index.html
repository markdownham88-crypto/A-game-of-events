TAP TO RE-WEAVE THE CLOTH

A Game of Events

Iain Kerr's event, the Situationist situation, and the act of rebranding — with an answer to whether the agency we protect must be visible, or only retained in its conditions

Mark Downham · for Daniel Monroy · London · 23 November 2026
Prologue · The Question and the Test

Visible, or Retained?

Daniel, you set a test before you asked your question, and the test deserves to go first. We reached a similar dynamic from different starting points. Mine came through counter-cultural philosophy and complexity. Yours came through the practical work of structure, diagnosis and operations on the brand side. You named both readings of that convergence. Either the pattern is more than one field's jargon, or we have each translated the other into the weave and made the match cheaper than it looks. Then you said where the real test lies: where our accounts predict differently.

In a Situation the constructor withdraws, while the weaver reappears in the cloth. Does the agency you're protecting need to be visible there, or only retained in its conditions?Daniel Monroy · November 2026

I want to answer it properly, which means not answering it at once. Since your note, a third voice has joined the conversation and changed its terms. Iain Kerr, writing for Emergent Futures Lab and its Worldmakers community, has spent six weeks testing what follows if events are taken as ontologically primary. Things are events. Thoughts are events. So are ways of sensing, feeling, doing and knowing. The subject who might hold, show or withdraw agency is also an event, a creative achievement of a dense knot of other events. If Kerr is right, your question changes shape. It is no longer about where a pre-existing agency sits, on the surface or in the substrate. It is about what kind of event can make agency happen at all, and keep it answerable.

So this reply has three strands: Kerr's event, the Situationists' situation, and your act of rebranding. I weave them, set them against one another, and then come back to your question with something more exact than a clever reply. Every diagram is an instrument rather than an illustration. You can drop a diver, take a cut through a brand's history, flatten a wordmark, push the stakes, and watch what each account predicts. I end with a table of divergent predictions, because that is your test, and a convergence that never risks a prediction is only a rhyme.

Plate I · Card of Suspicion

Three Words for One Shape?

Begin with suspicion, the kind you aim at the field of brands. DiMaggio and Powell's institutional isomorphism describes rational actors under shared pressures growing more alike, each making a locally sensible choice. You use it to explain why wordmarks converge. It applies just as well to concepts. Practitioners in neighbouring fields read the same small library: Whitehead, Deleuze, Stacey, Juarrero, Snowden, Massumi. They attend the same calls and share the same audience. Over time they converge on a vocabulary of emergence, relation, process and weave. That convergence may be mimetic rather than discovered.

A rhyme differs from a finding in three ways, and I hold myself to all three.

Independent derivation. Did each account reach the shape from its own materials? Yours came from rename studies, logo-commitment experiments and AI-purchasing surveys. Mine came from the salon, the cabaret and the dérive. Kerr's came from swimming pools, struts of spruce and the long event of flight. Those are three different archives, which is promising.

Shared anomalies. Do the accounts struggle with the same awkward facts? They do. Each has trouble with the case where an apparently small change in surface produces a change in kind for one participant and not another: Walsh, Winterich and Mittal's committed consumers, Kerr's diver at five hundred feet, the salon guest who leaves converted while another leaves bored.

Divergent predictions. Does each account forbid something the others allow? This is where translation-convergence fails, and where Plate XIV goes.

Plate II · Card of the Event

Kerr's Event

Emergent Futures Lab begins from a refusal. For nearly a century, it argues, creativity has been treated as if it starts in the head: individual, idea-centred, human. EFL proposes instead that creativity emerges from the middle of doing, out of the relations among processes, agents, tools, materials and environments, and that it is always more than human. A line attributed to Kerr compresses this: novelty emerges not from our heads but from the middle of action.

The event is the unit of that middle. In an early volume of the Emerging Futures newsletter, the lab built the concept diagrammatically. A process is a repeating loop of activity. When several processes converge and assemble, a unique event forms. The event's assemblage has an emergent logic that exceeds its parts. Feedback loops stabilise a field of potential whose stable outcomes are attractors. And one can make cuts into the process: slices at a given moment that give a portrait of a network, compared across time to watch processes enter and leave.

Notice this early, because everything later depends on it: an identity is a cut. A logo, a name, a one-line description is a slice through the brand-event, taken at a moment and then held still. Your insight that a first identity "was not wrong later. It was late" is a theorem about cuts. The slice was true to the network it portrayed. The network moved on and the slice did not. The first instrument makes that visible.

Flight invented the Wright brothers

The newer volumes push the concept to its most provocative edge. In The Event's Perspective, Frasca and Kerr ask what happens to innovation if we look at it from the side of the event. Their answer reverses the ordinary causal arrow. The Wright brothers did not invent flight. Flight, a long event of a few hundred years made of people, wood, wind and tools, coming together the way a storm does, invented them. The brothers became helpmates rather than originators. The authors guard against the obvious objection that this is personification. The invention is not a puppet master placing ideas in human heads. It is what they call, after Massumi, self-conditioning emergence.

Two features of this view decide what follows. First, agency is relational and conditioned, not abolished. Humans still matter, but the event transforms how they matter. Wood, wind and cloth count as collaborators, not passive stock. Second, the event has no clean start or finish. Flight is not over. Events do not begin when announced or end when launched. They cross thresholds and persist while their conditions hold.

The water

Then comes the test case Kerr has used for years. Sea water, the lab wrote in 2021, is a liquid when I float in it. I can dive into it and it affords me a gradual slowing, so I come back to the surface alive. Jump from four hundred feet and the ocean gives no such welcome. From that height the ocean is solid. Later volumes give this kind of experience a name, the transjective: relevance arising through the agent's interaction with the world. The same water is a buoyant liquid to the swimmer, a skimmable surface to the water strider, a crowd of marbles to the bacterium. The chemistry has not changed. The achieved quality has. And, crucially, the quality of the water and the quality of the diver's experience co-emerge from the same event. How we feel is not held in us alone, and how things are is not held in them alone.

Ontogenesis, not epistemology

From this Kerr draws the consequence that matters most for your question. If subject and world co-emerge in events, then changing how we sense, feel, do and know is not mainly a matter of updating beliefs about a fixed reality. It is a matter of experimentally co-creating novel events out of and with existing ones. In his phrasing, it is an ontogenetic question and not simply an epistemological one. EFL treats ontology phenomenologically, as the question of how anything comes to show up as a thing at all.

Plate III · Card of the Constructed Moment

The Constructed Situation

When the Situationist International published its definitions in the first number of its journal in 1958, the founding term was the constructed situation. It was defined as a moment of life deliberately constructed through the collective organisation of a unitary ambience and a game of events, un jeu d'événements.

The definition already contains the word event. The SI did not oppose the situation to the event. It defined the situation as an organisation of events: a game, played deliberately and collectively, staged in an ambience built for the purpose. If Kerr's event is the world's generative weather, the Situationist situation is an attempt to seed that weather, to arrange conditions so that a storm of intensified life forms where otherwise there would be only the drizzle of the spectacle.

Debord's 1957 Report on the Construction of Situations was frank about the roles involved. A situation is made to be lived by those who construct it. Early on, a directing hand may be needed to coordinate. The aim was for the passive "public" to shrink and for a new kind of participant, people who live the situation rather than watch it, to grow. The constructor exists, and the constructor's ambition is to dissolve into the constructors.

In All Coherence Gone I traced this shape back through the cabaret to the salon and called it the self-dissolving frame: a structure built deliberately to hide its own framing, which reaches its highest purpose when it disappears into what it frames. The salonnière arranged bodies and conversation as a field of force. Le Chat Noir detonated that field into fumisme. The Situationists armed it with the dérive and détournement, and set it against the total mediation of life by images that Debord called the spectacle.

Event and situation, side by side

The two concepts share a lot. Both are relational. Both reject the spectator who stands outside and receives a finished world. They differ on three points, and each difference matters for your question.

Constructedness. Kerr's event is the ontological default: everything is events, constructed or not. The SI's situation is a made event, a deliberate intervention against a world whose default events have been degraded into spectacle.

The politics of the default. Kerr's ontology is generous: flight, smartphones, sails and Sedna are all creative achievements. The SI's is suspicious: in late capitalism the default event is a pseudo-event, organised by the commodity for spectators. An ontology in which everything is creative event struggles to name the events that are degraded, captured or recuperated. EFL does have a resource here, its concept of explaining away, and Plate XI presses on it.

Ephemerality. The SI built situations to pass, and their destiny was supersession or recuperation. Kerr's events persist while their conditions hold. The SI does not ask how a situation endures. Kerr asks little about how an event is captured.

A rebrand sits exactly where these differences meet. It is a constructed intervention in a long-running event, it must endure, and it lives entirely inside the economy the Situationists named.

Plate IV · Card of the Sponsored Moment

The Rebrand as an Act

Your Part Two does not use the language of events, but it is closer to Kerr than it looks, and further from Debord than it would like.

You split "the brand" into three layers that age at different speeds. Story is what the business is, whom it serves and where it sits. Language is its name and the words for what it sells. Expression is mark, type and palette. In EFL's vocabulary these are three converging processes with different tempos, and the brand is the event their convergence forms, held together by recognition, repetition and the habits of readers.

You distinguish a refresh, which keeps the story and retunes the expression, from a rebrand, which changes the story the identity has to carry. You read the rename literature carefully. Horsky and Swyngedouw's renamed firms improved, but not because of the name: the rename signalled real change to come. Kashmiri and Mahajan found markets rewarded names that signalled a future change of scope over names that merely caught up. You add frequency and commitment: committed consumers punish redesigns that uncommitted ones welcome. You name a third reader, AI systems that read not the mark but the record. From this you derive an order of operations, story then language then expression, set against the Amputation Playbook, which starts at expression and never asks whether the story moved.

An act, not an event

Your account is an account of an act, and I mean that as a compliment before I mean it as a criticism. Its grammatical subject is the leadership team. It assumes a diagnostician who can stand at the edge of the brand-event, cut it, read the layers, and sequence an intervention. That is the constructor's posture, and the constructor's discipline is real: someone has to sign off the spend.

So the rebrand-as-act sits between Kerr and Debord. Like the situation, it is a deliberate construction. Unlike it, it is meant to last and is staged inside the spectacle rather than against it. Like Kerr's event, it lives in a long becoming. Unlike it, it has a sponsor, a budget line and a launch date. The next instrument lets you place any made moment in that field and read off what it inherits from each corner.

Plate V · Card of the Water

The Transjective Brand

This is the first place where Kerr's water and your evidence meet exactly enough to be more than rhyme.

Walsh, Winterich and Mittal redesigned logos for existing athletic-shoe brands and measured responses by commitment. The most committed consumers reacted most negatively. The weakly committed reacted positively. You read this as an exposure question in two parts: how often do they see it, and how attached are they?

Read it through the water. The redesign is the "same" relational event. To the uncommitted reader it arrives as liquid: it parts around them, slows them gently, and they surface to say the new look is fresh. To the committed reader the same redesign arrives at terminal velocity, because commitment is velocity: years of accumulated approach, a body that has been falling toward this mark for a decade. For that reader the water has turned solid. There is no gradual slowing, only impact.

Commitment is height · the loyal reader hits the mark at terminal velocity

Your third reader has a place in the same water. If the loyal human is the diver at five hundred feet, the AI system is the water strider. It does not plunge into the brand at all. It skates on the surface tension of the record: names, descriptions, and whether the accounts agree. A record full of contradictions is a surface with broken tension, and the strider falls through. The fourth reader, the bacterium for which water is a crowd of bouncing marbles, is the micro-encounter: one footer, one unrevised template, one supplier PDF, one search query. At that scale the brand is not an identity but a scatter of collisions. Rebrands fail here constantly and invisibly. The instrument below runs all four.

This mapping changes a claim in your paper. You write that a loyal, frequent audience will punish a visual change more than a casual one, and that a system reading the record responds to a language change on a different clock. That reads as two audiences reacting differently to one change. The transjective reading says something stronger: there is no one change. There are as many rebrands as there are kinds of encounter, and each is real. "The rebrand" as a single object assessed by a leadership team is a cut taken from the sponsor's height, and the sponsor's height is only one more reader.

Plate VI · Card of the Threshold

Degree and Kind

Your refresh/rebrand distinction is your sharpest tool, and EFL supplies its physics. The physical world, the lab wrote early on, is in continuous variation, always crossing critical thresholds: the egg turns from liquid to solid, still water boils, gas ignites. Near those points, change in degree becomes change in kind.

A refresh is a change in degree. A rebrand is a change in kind. You draw the line through the business: has the story moved? The event view adds that the threshold is relational. Boiling point depends on pressure. Whether a change of surface crosses into a change of kind depends on the reader's commitment, frequency and height. A tidier palette is a change of degree for the casual reader and can be a change of kind for the loyal one, who reads every touch to the mark as a touch to the story because, for them, mark and story fused long ago.

So your layers age at different speeds, as you say, but they are separable only for some readers. For the committed reader they have been woven so tight that pulling one thread moves the cloth. Each dot below is a reader, placed at their own boiling point. Slide the change across and watch who crosses into kind.

Your honest audit of the 7UP case is the right instrument at the right scale. In August 2026 Keurig Dr Pepper bundled a real reformulation with a vertical lockup and bolder colour. You ask whether the lockup is doing story work or only riding along. The event view adds a time axis. Riding along is not a permanent condition. An expression element that arrived with no story can, over eighteen months of encounter, acquire story and become what the loyal reader means by the brand's new self. Elements do not have fixed jobs. The event recruits them.

Plate VII · Card of the Knife

Blocking, Exaptation, Amputation

EFL has an account of creative method, not only creative ontology, and it sheds a cold light on the Amputation Playbook.

The method turns on blocking: setting aside an existing function so a thing can be used in new ways. Engage and block, experiment toward unintended capacities, then select and co-opt. Those unintended uses are exaptations, novel affordances. Two qualifications are crucial. Early experiments disclose an area's underlying logic: its habits, processes and environments. Only after that disclosure can the logic be blocked judiciously. Trying to block everything, the lab says, is hubris.

Put the Amputation Playbook beside this. It blocks, but blindly. It sets aside the mark's irregularities, the superscript, the drawn idiosyncrasy, the cue a competitor with budget could not copy, without first disclosing what logic those features carried. And it blocks for the wrong master: the infrastructure's habit (grid, icon template, design system) rather than the business's. It is total blocking, in the service of an existing logic rather than against one. It is the inverse of exaptation.

Exaptation finds the unintended use · amputation removes the unintended asset

Your remedy, starting with an inventory of what a competitor could not copy, is the lab's disclosure step in brand form. "The most useful output of an identity review is often a list of distinctive assets that exist and are underused" is close to a definition of latent exaptation. Here the convergence is real. You reached it from Romaniuk's distinctive-asset research. He reached it from the long event of flight. The derivations are independent.

The event view also says something less comfortable about your order of operations. EFL's loop is iterative: disclose, block, experiment, disclose again. Your sequence is linear. If disclosure happens through experiment, the story cannot be fully settled before expression is touched, because some of what the story is only shows up when the expression is played with. On the event view, step three sometimes teaches step one.

Plate VIII · Card of the Spectacle

The Launch as Pseudo-Event

Now Debord comes back, and he does not come back kindly.

Every rebrand culminates in a launch: the post, the reveal film, the line about evolution. You note that justified and unjustified rebrands look identical from outside because they arrive with the same apparatus. In Situationist terms the launch is a constructed moment. Its game of events is organised, its ambience designed, its timing set. It is a situation in every respect but one: it is made to be watched, not lived.

Daniel Boorstin, writing in 1961, named this kind of happening the pseudo-event: planned rather than spontaneous, staged mainly to be reported, ambiguously related to underlying reality, and tending to be self-fulfilling. The rebrand launch fits closely. It is planted in order to be reported. Its relation to the business is exactly the ambiguity you diagnose. And it hopes to fulfil itself: say "evolution" loudly enough and the market will believe the business evolved.

Debord goes deeper than Boorstin. For the SI, the spectacle is a social relation among people mediated by images, and within it even genuine change is experienced as image. A business that really has changed still has to announce its change as spectacle. Your 7UP problem, a real story change giving cover to an expression change, is a local case of a general law:

In the spectacle, the authentic is the best packaging for the inauthentic.

This is where your account and the Situationists' predict differently. Yours implies that a well-sequenced rebrand can escape the pseudo-event, so that the launch becomes an honest report of a real change. Theirs implies the launch stays a pseudo-event however true its content, because the form produces the passive reader. Kerr gives a third reading, the most useful of the three. If flight is not over, the brand-event did not begin at the launch and will not end there. What the brand becomes is decided in the years of encounters after it. The launch is weather. The brand is climate.

The spectacle owns the launch · the event owns the decade after it

Plate IX · Card of the Lure

Lure and Judgement

One more instrument from EFL, borrowed from Whitehead, reframes your four leadership questions. A recent volume takes up Whitehead's definition of a proposition as a lure for feeling. Ordinary logic asks whether a statement is true, and so turns readers into judges. But every statement also pulls, drawing us out of ourselves into new ways of feeling and acting. We often stop at judgement and miss the pull. Fact-checking, the lab notes, can confirm every claim false and still miss how the statements move people.

Your Part Two is a superb instrument of judgement. Has the story changed? Does each visual change carry the story or ride along? Is the record one story, in the buyer's words? These are truth-questions put to the rebrand's propositions. They are necessary, and on their own they miss the lure.

A new identity is a proposition offered to every reader at once, and its effect is mostly not a truth-effect. The vertical lockup claims nothing that could be checked. It pulls: it invites a posture, a speed, a way of reaching for the can. The flattened wordmark does not assert that the company is now generic. It lures the reader into treating it as generic, letting it slide past at the speed of every other flattened mark. The Amputation Playbook does its harm as a lure, not as a falsehood, which is why no dashboard measures it.

John Protevi, a recent Worldmakers guest, offers a test that works on lures. Does the encounter produce repeatable, mutually active joyous affect in care and cooperation? Does it increase the puissance, the capacity to act, of the bodies involved, so that they can form new empowering encounters beyond the first? Applied to a rebrand, the question becomes whether the new identity increases the capacity to act of the people who meet it: the loyal customer, the new buyer, the employee who must wear it, the supplier who must print it, and, to be thorough, the machine that must describe it. Some honest rebrands fail this test, and some dishonest refreshes pass it for a while. A leadership team needs both tests.

Plate X · Card of the Three Hands

Three Postures of Agency

Now I can come to your question with the instruments it needs. Your question names two figures: the constructor who withdraws and the weaver who reappears in the cloth. Kerr adds a third: the helpmate, whom the event invents. They are three postures of agency, and they differ on the one thing your question is about: where agency is located before, during and after the made moment.

The constructor's agency comes first. The salonnière, the Situationist director and the brand strategist exist before the situation, design its conditions and then, by choice, method or modesty, step back. Their agency is retained in the conditions: the seating plan, the ambience, the design system. This is the self-dissolving frame, and its ideal is the Dàodéjīng's ruler, of whom the people know only that there is one, and who leaves them saying the work happened by itself.

The weaver's agency co-arises. This is my principle, and I take it to be where Ralph Stacey was heading in Complexity and Group Processes: the weaver reappears in what is woven whilst weaving. In complex responsive processes, following Mead and Elias, the self is itself a pattern of the ongoing conversation. The weaver cannot withdraw, because there is nowhere outside the cloth to stand.

The helpmate's agency is conditioned. Kerr's Wright brothers neither stood outside flight and arranged it nor merely co-arose with it. They were invented by it and swept in. They became careful participants in a becoming that came before them and exceeded them. The weaver belongs to the loom. The helpmate belongs to the weather.

Your question is posed between the first two postures. It asks whether the constructor's withdrawn agency, retained in conditions, is enough, or whether it must appear as the weaver's does, in the cloth. At first Kerr's third posture seems to dissolve the question. If agency is always conditioned by the event, it is neither visible nor retained, and there is nothing outside the event for it to be retained in. I think that is half right, and the danger lies in the half that is wrong.

Plate XI · Card of the Alibi

The Danger in the Event

Chris Corrigan's recent letter to his Art of Hosting community is the sharpest text I know on this danger, and the corrective my own All Coherence Gone needed. After nearly a quarter-century of hosting, he writes that "our methods may look like they fade the power of the host into the background." The architecture still carries that power. Someone called the meeting, framed the purpose and chose the harvest. He offers a triangle of three powers that meet in any container: interactions (distributed power), structure (contextual power: mandates, resources, constraints) and design (the host's privileged power over purpose, method and invitation). And he warns that a powerful experience is not system change.

Read Corrigan beside Kerr's flight and an uncomfortable symmetry appears. The self-dissolving frame says the host withdrew and the participants did it themselves. The event ontology can be made to say the host was invented by the event. Both may be true. Used carelessly, both perform the same operation: they remove the address to which a question about power could be delivered.

EFL has the word for this. Its own term is explaining away: apparently acknowledging something's importance while actually dismissing it. An event ontology that says "humans still matter, but transformatively conditioned" can, in the wrong hands, explain away human answerability. Kerr's hands are not the wrong hands. His insistence on care, attunement and Protevi's ethical test shows that. But a concept does not carry its author's ethics when it travels, and this one will travel into boardrooms. A strategist who has read The Event's Perspective and then flattened a sixty-year-old wordmark can say, with philosophical sophistication, that the design system invented them. The redescription would be accurate, and it would be a laundering.

The constructor's alibi was always the weather.

So Kerr does not dissolve your question. He relocates it. If agency is an achievement of events, then answerability is too. It does not exist by default. It has to be made to show up, in EFL's own phenomenological sense of ontology: how does any thing come to show up as some thing at all? Answerability is one of those things, and it shows up only in events constructed so that it can.

Plate XII · Card of the Spine

道 理 勢

勢

The classical Chinese tradition anticipated this problem with unusual exactness, and its vocabulary gives your question a structure the Western terms lack.

勢 shì is the propensity of a configuration: the potential stored in an arrangement, as in water poised above a gorge, a crossbow drawn, a slope. Sunzi's chapter on shì tells the skilled commander to seek victory in the configuration and not to demand it of individual men. Then comes the image that matters here:

故善戰人之勢，如轉圓石於千仞之山者，勢也

"So the shì of one skilled at sending men into battle is like rolling a round stone down a mountain a thousand ren high. That is shì." · Sūnzǐ, ch. 5 · my rendering

A thousand ren is the height from which water becomes stone. Sunzi's boulder and Kerr's diver obey the same physics. Shì is agency retained in conditions in its purest form. The general who masters it no longer needs to be seen commanding, because the slope commands. It is the strategic ancestor of the self-dissolving frame, the design system and the hosting container.

理 lǐ is pattern: originally the veins in jade, the grain the carver follows, and later the inner principle by which things cohere. Lǐ is the weaver's reappearance: the pattern in the cloth that carries the pressure of the hand that wove it. A distinctive brand asset is lǐ, the irregularity left by a particular history of making, which no template can supply because no template went through that history.

道 dào is the way, the generative course in which configurations form and patterns appear. It is the nearest classical analogue to Kerr's event field.

功成事遂，百姓皆謂：我自然

"When the work is done and the matter accomplished, the people all say: we are simply like this." · Dàodéjīng 17

Reading the spine at rest

This plate is the argument before it moves. It is a cut through the instrument that follows, held still so it can be read slowly, the way an identity holds a brand-event still. Every placement is meant to carry weight, so here is how to read it.

The circle is 道. Dào sits at the crown, but it is also the whole ring: the event field inside which every configuration forms and every pattern appears. I have tagged it with Kerr's name because his event is the nearest modern analogue to the classical way, a becoming that comes before and exceeds every particular arrangement. Nothing in this plate stands outside the circle. The constructor and the weaver are both inside it, which is Kerr's point and Stacey's: there is no external standpoint from which to arrange the cloth.

The two lower glyphs are the two postures your question names. On the right, in red, is 勢 shì: the propensity of conditions, the constructor's art, the design system and the hosting container. Its gloss ends in retained, because this is agency held in the arrangement. It is the round stone poised on the mountain, doing its work without being seen. On the left, in gold, is 理 lǐ: pattern in the cloth, the weaver's reappearance, the distinctive asset. Its gloss ends in visible, because this is agency showing itself as grain. The colours are deliberate. Red is the colour of power and of the fold-axis that runs through this page. Gold is the warmth of the hand.

The two sit at the same height, and that matters. Neither outranks the other inside the field of dào. A tradition that ranked shì above lǐ produced the Legalist ruler who governs by arrangement and is never seen. One that ranked lǐ above shì would produce pure spectacle: pattern with no conditions behind it, the founder-as-brand, the mark as decoration. The plate keeps them level because the answer to your question is not a choice between them.

The answer is the dotted line. The arc runs from shì to lǐ, and the words above it are the whole thesis compressed: shì must be able to show its lǐ. Three features of that line carry the argument.

It is dotted, not solid. A solid line would mean standing visibility: the configuration always on show, the host forever on stage, and therefore spectacle. A missing line would be Corrigan's hidden power, conditions with no traceable author. The dotted line is the third term, legibility: a connection that exists, can be followed when it is needed, and does not insist on being looked at.

It arches upward, toward 道. The route from configuration to pattern does not go around the event field. It passes up through it. Legibility is not something the constructor owns and hands over. It is an event in its own right, co-created inside the field. That is the ontogenetic turn of Plate XIII: answerability has to be built, occasion by occasion, as part of the event.

It runs from shì to lǐ, not the reverse. The question is never whether pattern can be traced back to conditions; a good eye can always do that. The question is whether the arrangement can be made to show itself as pattern when the water turns hard, at five hundred feet, where money moves. The arc's direction is the direction of accountability.

Plate XIII · Card of the Answer

The Answer

My answer has four parts.

1 · The dichotomy assumes the wrong ontology

"Visible or retained in its conditions" assumes an agency that already exists and then either shows itself or hides behind the scaffold. On Kerr's account, and on Stacey's, no such agency exists before the event. Agency is not a possession to be displayed or stored. It is achieved, or not, in each encounter, the way the water's liquidity is.

2 · Asked at the right height, it has a velocity-dependent answer

When the encounter is slow and low-stakes (the swimmer at forty feet, the casual reader, the hosted conversation that only explores), agency retained in the conditions is enough. It parts around the participants and buoys them. Shì suffices. When the encounter is fast and high-stakes (the diver at five hundred feet, the committed reader, the moment in your own data where money moves and delegation stops), the same conditions arrive as a solid surface. Agency that existed only in the configuration becomes the thing people hit. At that height it must show itself, or the participant is broken against an arrangement no one will own.

Shì at forty feet. Lǐ at five hundred.

3 · The third term: not visible, but legible

So the answer to "visible or retained?" is neither. It is legible: retained in the conditions but traceable on demand, able to surface as visible pattern at the moment the event turns solid. Standing visibility is its own trap. Debord saw that agency which must always be seen becomes image, and therefore spectacle: the founder-as-brand, the host forever on stage, the CEO in every launch film. Retention without traceability is the other trap, the one Corrigan names. Legibility is the narrow path between performance and concealment.

The word stings. James C. Scott made legibility the name of the state's project of making populations readable from above, so that they could be taxed, conscripted and planned. I use it in reverse, deliberately. Scott's legibility is the legibility of the governed to power. Mine is the legibility of power to the governed: of the host to the hosted, of the design system to the people its flattening affects, of the constructor to the participants who could not see the construction. Scott's legibility looks down. This one looks up.

4 · Legibility must itself be built, ontogenetically

The final turn is Kerr's. In an event ontology, legibility cannot be a property the constructor has and chooses to disclose. It has to be an event in its own right, co-created with the participants, so that answerability can show up when it is needed. In practice that means building into any made moment, whether a hosted gathering, a constructed situation or a rebrand, its own occasions of disclosure: moments where the frame shows itself on purpose, so that the shì can be read as lǐ and questioned. Détournement is one such practice. Corrigan's triangle is another. Your element-by-element audit of a bundled launch is a third.

So, plainly: the agency I am protecting does not need to be visible in the Situation. It needs to be retained in its conditions in a way that can be made to appear: shì that can show its lǐ, a constructor who can be found when the water turns hard. In an event ontology even that capacity is not given. It has to be co-created as part of the event. A Situation that cannot produce, on demand, an account of who arranged it is not a liberated situation. It is a spectacle that has learned to hide its own staging.

Plate XV · Card of Practice

A Practice, If One Is Wanted

For a leadership team approaching a change of identity with all three strands in mind, here is a practice. It doesn't replace your four questions. It surrounds them.

Cut at several times. Following EFL's method, take slices of the brand-event at its founding, at its last change and now (Instrument II). The story has moved if the processes converging on the brand have changed, not if leadership feels it has.

Disclose before you block. Before setting aside any feature of the existing identity, run experiments that reveal what logic it carries. Treat every irregularity as a possible exaptation until shown otherwise (Instrument VII).

Map the readers by height. For each product line, place readers on Kerr's water: swimmers, divers, striders, bacteria. Ask what the change will be for each, not what the change is (Instruments V and VI).

Weigh lures as well as truths. After asking whether each element carries the story, ask what it pulls the reader toward. Apply Protevi's test: does the new identity increase the capacity to act of the people who must meet it?

Make the construction legible. Build occasions of disclosure into the launch and after it. Name the people who decided. Let the shì show its lǐ (Instrument XII).

Watch the decade, not the day. The launch is weather. Track what the ride-along elements become over eighteen months, and be ready for step three to have taught step one something the sequence did not anticipate.

Plate XVI · Card of Theses

Sixteen Theses

  1. An identity is a cut.A slice through a brand-event, held still. It was not wrong later; it was late. Rebranding takes a new cut. Refreshing re-inks the old one.
  2. The situation is an event with an organiser.The SI defined the constructed situation as a game of events. Kerr's event needs no organiser. The rebrand is a game of events played inside the spectacle and meant to last.
  3. There is no single rebrand.There are as many rebrands as there are heights of encounter, and each is real: liquid to the casual reader, solid to the committed one, a surface to the machine, collisions to the footer.
  4. Commitment is height.The loyal reader meets the redesigned mark at terminal velocity.
  5. The threshold is relational.The line between refresh and rebrand, degree and kind, moves with the reader's commitment, as boiling point moves with pressure.
  6. Amputation is blocking without disclosure.Exaptation finds the unintended use. The Playbook removes the unintended asset, which EFL rightly calls hubris.
  7. The authentic packages the inauthentic.In the spectacle, a real story change gives cover to a riding expression change, and the bundled launch is built for that.
  8. Every identity is a lure.Judging its truth is necessary and not sufficient. The flattened mark does its harm as a lure.
  9. Three postures, not two.The constructor is prior and withdraws. The weaver co-arises and is re-patterned. The helpmate is invented by the event. The third relocates your question without dissolving it.
  10. The event can be an alibi."The design system invented me" may be true and may be a laundering. Answerability is not given in an event world. It has to be co-created.
  11. Shì without lǐ is hidden power.Agency retained in configuration with no pattern to show for it: the ruler who governs by arrangement and is never seen to act.
  12. Not visible, but legible.Retained in its conditions and able to surface as pattern when the water turns hard. This is legibility looking up, not Scott's legibility looking down.
  13. Shì at forty feet, lǐ at five hundred.Where money moves and delegation stops, there must be a hand that can be found.
  14. The launch is weather; the brand is climate.The spectacle owns the day of the reveal. The event owns the decade after it.
  15. Our convergence is partly real.Distinctive assets as latent exaptations, reached independently. It is partly cheap: the machine reader, where we agree for reasons that do not discriminate between us.
  16. And genuinely divergent.On separability, sequence and the neutrality of diagnosis. That is where the evidence should go.
